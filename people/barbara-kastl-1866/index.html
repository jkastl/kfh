The 1922 Emporia Weekly Gazette article reprinted on pp. 31-33 ("Raised On A Western Farm") is about Barbara, as Mrs. Albert Mares. It names the four sons John, Joe, Adolph and Anton, who then ran the farm.

From the book

Mrs. Albert Mares, who lives two miles from Emporia on East Logan Avenue, is an interesting and wholesome type of the foreign-born woman who never has discarded the Gospel of Work brought with her from her old home in Austria-Hungary but who, none the less, is a thorough American. She came with her parents to the prairies of Nebraska when she was 6 years old, and can recall that she had started to school in the old country, and that she brought with her her primer in the Bohemian language. And that is about all she can recall until the trip across seas, then the sod house on the prairie, in which her father and mother reared their large family.

p. 31

"I might have held that job a long time," says Mrs. Mares, "but old country folks thought a girl must be married by the time she was 18, and so, at 18, I was married, in the same sod house on my parents' homestead in which they had started life on the prairie. My father had built a new house, of lumber, but they hadn't moved into it. My mother was afraid the wind would blow it away. My wedding dress was red changeable silk, with an overskirt, trimmed with black silk lace. A friend of mine helped me make it. I had bought a sewing machine while I was working, and learned to sew. My girls can all sew well, and are good housekeepers.

p. 32

"My parents came to America." says Mrs. Mares, "as did so many old country people, to get their sons aways from the army. My father and mother had a hard time, and just as my life has been easier than was my mother's, so my children's life is easier than mine has been. It is hard for us old country people to learn new ways, and we are likely to think old ways the best. But we have to learn that what were the best ways in the old country are not always the best ways here. And the quicker we learn this, and apply it, the quicker we become happy and satisfied, instead of longing for the old country. My mother is still living, well along in the eighties. For years she was postmaster, the postoffice being in her little sod house. Always she wished for better things, and always she worked hard for the, and taught her children to work for them. She kept her children thinking of the church, too, as I have tried to keep mine. We can't be very bad if we love our church. I never have been sick—I think it is because I have been outdoors so much. I have worked any place on the farm I was needed, and have been glad I could do it, glad I had the strength to do it. I am proud of my good, strong boys and girls, and of our home and of all we have been able to accomplish."

p. 33
