There were three cooks, six dining room girls, two ironers, a wash woman, two dishwashers, janitor, head waiter, bell boys, night and day porters—a small army of help. BUt it didn't take much to feed them, says Mrs. Mares, as the help was supposed to eat what was left when the paying guests were through, and sometimes it was mighty slim pickings. Often big special midnight suppers were served, and the help worked right through till all was finished, with no over time—but at such times they all had a big feed, as there was certain to be a lot of food left. The landlord kept an eye on the kitchen, usually, and there was no chance for the help to eat on the sly, as he was likely to bob in any minute.

The women help did their washing and sewing at night, in the little time they had left after their long day's work. And they did each others work, in turns, that all might have occasional opportunity to go to church.

"I might have held that job a long time," says Mrs. Mares, "but old country folks thought a girl must be married by the time she was 18, and so, at 18, I was married, in the same sod house on my parents' homestead in which they had started life on the prairie. My father had built a new house, of lumber, but they hadn't moved into it. My mother was afraid the wind would blow it away. My wedding dress was red changeable silk, with an overskirt, trimmed with black silk lace. A friend of mine helped me make it. I had bought a sewing machine while I was working, and learned to sew. My girls can all sew well, and are good housekeepers.

"Mr. Mares and I lived on a part of his father's land, but here again, everything was new, and the prairie was so bare. We built what we needed, a little four-room house, and when the other part of the farm was sold, we sold too. We lived several place in Nebraska, always trying to get ahead, always planning so that life might be better for our children than it had been for us. Some years we did well, and others it was hard work making a living, for we had to fight all the bad things that come to farmers."

Ten years ago the Mareses came to Lyon County to visit their daughter who, with her husband, lived on a farm near Miller. Also, they visited the Anton Ptaceks, who had been their neighbors in Nebraska. The Mareses liked Lyon County and after considerable looking around, they bought the old Laupheimer farm, of 170 acres. The winters are long and cold in Nebraska, and they felt they would like to live in a warmer climate. Here, they have met many discouragements in the way of dry weather and high water and all the ills that have hit Kansas farmers in the past nine years. But they wouldn't care to return to Nebraska, and they wouldn't consider leaving the farm. They wished to nearer schools and church and the location of the Laupheimer farm suited them exactly.

The Mareses went home to Nebraska after their visit, and the next spring came here to live. The new farm was badly run down, the house had burned a year or two earlier, many of the trees had been destroyed and the orchard had mostly died out. The family faced a big job of getting the place cleaned up, the soil into better producing condition, a new house, and all of the thousand and one things that confront thrifty farmers on a run-down place. A small house had been moved to the farm after the big house burned, and in this the family lived until 1917, when they built their present handsome, convenient residence.

Always Mrs. Mares has done a great deal of work on the farm, aside from her housekeeping and baby-tending. She has reared ten children, six daughters and four sons, and one baby died in infancy. Five of the daughters are married: the youngest, Lydia, was graduated from the Emporia High School last year, and the four sons, John, Joe, Adolph and Anton, run the farm. Mr. Mares has put out a new orchard, and spends much time with his trees. He hopes to have great deal of fruit on the place within the next few years, provided the late springs don't kill it.

"I couldn't be happy without my chickens and other poultry," says Mrs. Mares. "I make a lot of money off of them, too. Last year I raised 100 geese, 300 chickens, some turkeys, guineas and ducks, but geese and chickens are my principal poultry crop. I sell many eggs, too, though we probably eat more than we sell. My geese bring good prices, and I dress them and have the feathers for my own use. I have given each of my married daughters two featherbeds and six pillows, and I am making like outfits for my sons and for my daughter at home."

"Mother strips her feathers, too," said the daughter. Lydia, "and that makes them just like down." Mrs. Mares brought two boxes in which she ws preparing feathers for pillows. One box contained the striped feathers, light as down, indeed, and as soft and fluffy, the other box containing the spines of the feathers, carefully stripped of the down. Wonderful beds such feathers make, and wonderful is the patience that goes into the making of them.

"Mother furnished this house with her goose money," said Lydia, "and we buy so many things with poultry money that we have decided that poultry pays better than anything else on the farm. Mother puts in the greater part of her time with the poultry."

Lydia, since the marriage of the next youngest daughter, last year, has taken over a large part of the housekeeping.

Mrs. Mares had Rhode Island Red and White Leghorn chickens—a great egg and table combination.
